% Introduction（草稿）
Sports outcome forecasting has attracted sustained attention from
statistics, machine learning, and, more recently, large language
models (LLMs). The task is attractive because outcomes are public,
structured, and frequent, and because a predictive model can be
evaluated not only on accuracy but also on downstream financial
consequences in betting markets. However, the same properties make
football forecasting a demanding test bed: betting markets aggregate
vast amounts of information into closing odds, and any method that
hopes to add value must do so \emph{against} a strong, continuously
updated baseline.

The literature has moved in two directions. On the modeling side,
classical approaches---Poisson and Dixon--Coles models, Elo ratings,
and gradient-boosted trees---provide strong and interpretable
baselines. On the representation side, LLM-based forecasting
systems~\citep{halawi2024approaching, alur2025aia, yang2025llm}
demonstrate that language models can produce calibrated probability
forecasts when given structured evidence. Yet most published
evaluations in the LLM forecasting line measure accuracy and Brier
scores in isolation, without a market baseline, without explicit
leakage controls, and without a betting protocol that would make the
financial claims reproducible.

A separate literature on selective prediction and classification
with a reject option~\citep{chow1970optimum, geifman2017selective}
has established that a predictor may abstain on a subset of inputs,
trading coverage against risk. In betting markets this distinction
matters more than accuracy: a forecast that is accurate on average is
not necessarily actionable, because the market prices its own
certainty into the odds. A model that never beats the market can
still identify which matches the market itself is most uncertain
about---and abstention on those matches may be the only decision
with positive expected value. This paper studies that distinction:
predicting the \emph{difficulty} of a match, rather than claiming an
edge over the market.

In this paper, these gaps are addressed with a deliberately conservative
evaluation design. The contributions are as follows.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Contribution 1: a leakage-controlled benchmark.}
Closing-market probabilities remain a very strong upper benchmark:
under a strictly leakage-controlled protocol (pre-match features
only, train-only statistics, chronological split, manual
verification), no learned method exceeds de-vigged closing odds
(54.2\% accuracy, 0.967 log loss) on a temporally held-out test
period of 1,104 matches, and a domain-enhanced LLM matches
market-level probability calibration at negligible cost.
Market-derived features account for approximately 87\% of the
aggregate permutation importance under the XGBoost specification.
\item \textbf{Contribution 2: difficulty is predictable even when
outcomes are not.} Outcome predictability and difficulty
predictability are different objects. The market cannot be beaten
($\mathrm{E}[\mathrm{edge}] \approx 0$), yet the probability of a
correct prediction is strongly heterogeneous and identifiable ex
ante: the market's own probability level stratifies accuracy from
40.1\% to 75.3\%, whereas odds movement and bookmaker dispersion do
not (they are confounded with market strength), and an uncertainty
index adds roughly ten percentage points of stratification beyond
market strength. A no-bet policy on high-uncertainty matches
improves risk-adjusted returns.
\item \textbf{Contribution 3: decision-level uncertainty control.}
Prediction confidence should not be interpreted as betting edge, but
uncertainty can be used to decide when to abstain. Value-based
selection of high-confidence bets loses money (ROI $-11.5\%$,
$-7.1\%$ after calibration); error rates among high-confidence
predictions exceed the market's (22.3\% vs 16.0\% at confidence
$\geq 0.7$), and the no-bet rule driven by the uncertainty index is
an abstention policy whose threshold is a coverage control in the
selective-prediction sense---trading coverage against risk rather
than against accuracy. All financial evaluations share one betting
protocol (equal-stake and fractional-Kelly on a single bookmaker's
closing odds, bootstrap confidence intervals, coverage reporting,
and no-bet decisions separated from executed bets), and the full
pipeline is public and script-driven.
\end{itemize}
