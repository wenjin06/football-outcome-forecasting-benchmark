% Introduction (draft)
Efficient betting markets aggregate vast amounts of information into
closing odds that are notoriously difficult to beat: across a large
body of sports-forecasting research, models that outperform the
market's own probabilities---on accuracy, let alone on
profitability---are rare and fragile. Efficiency of this kind is not
a nuisance for forecasting research; it is the environment in which
any honest evaluation must be conducted, and it sharpens the
question a forecasting paper can credibly answer.

Under such conditions the central forecasting problem shifts from
\emph{predicting outcomes} to \emph{predicting when predictions are
unreliable}. Sports outcome forecasting has attracted
sustained attention from statistics, machine learning, and, more
recently, large language models (LLMs) because outcomes are public,
structured, and frequent, and because a predictive model can be
evaluated not only on accuracy but also on downstream financial
consequences in betting markets. The literature has moved in two
directions. On the modeling side, classical approaches---Poisson and
Dixon--Coles models, Elo ratings, and gradient-boosted
trees---provide strong and interpretable baselines. On the
representation side, LLM-based forecasting
systems~\citep{halawi2024approaching, alur2025aia, yang2025llm}
demonstrate that language models can produce calibrated probability
forecasts when given structured evidence. Yet most published
evaluations in the LLM forecasting line measure accuracy and Brier
scores in isolation, without a market baseline, without explicit
leakage controls, and without a betting protocol that would make the
financial claims reproducible.

A separate literature on selective prediction and classification
with a reject option~\citep{chow1970optimum, geifman2017selective}
has established that a predictor may abstain on a subset of inputs,
trading coverage against risk. In betting markets this distinction
matters more than accuracy: a forecast that is accurate on average is
not necessarily actionable, because the market prices its own
certainty into the odds. A model that never beats the market can
still identify which matches the market itself is most uncertain
about---and abstention on those matches may be the only decision
with positive expected value. This paper studies that distinction.
\emph{Forecast difficulty} is defined as the ex-ante probability that
a forecasting system produces an incorrect outcome prediction; the
term refers to the reliability of the system's own decision, not to
any colloquial property of the match such as strong versus weak
teams or close versus one-sided games. Predicting difficulty rather
than claiming an edge over the market is then a well-posed
forecasting problem of its own, and one that remains tractable
precisely where outcome prediction does not.

Football is used as the empirical environment because its markets
are deep, liquid, and continuously updated, and because match
outcomes are public, structured, and frequent. The questions above
are addressed with a deliberately conservative evaluation design.
The contributions are as follows.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Contribution 1: predictive edge disappears in
efficient betting markets.} Under a strictly leakage-controlled
protocol (pre-match features only, train-only statistics,
chronological split, manual verification), no learned method exceeds
de-vigged closing odds (54.2\% accuracy, 0.967 log loss) on a
temporally held-out test period of 1,104 matches, and a
domain-enhanced LLM matches market-level probability calibration at
negligible cost. Value-based selection of high-confidence bets
produces consistently negative returns, and market-derived features
account for approximately 87\% of the aggregate permutation
importance under the XGBoost specification.
\item \textbf{Contribution 2: forecast reliability remains
heterogeneous and predictable even when outcomes are not.}
Outcome predictability and difficulty predictability are different
objects. The market cannot be beaten ($\mathrm{E}[\mathrm{edge}]
\approx 0$), yet the ex-ante probability of a correct prediction is
strongly heterogeneous and identifiable: the market's own probability
level stratifies accuracy from 40.1\% to 75.3\%, whereas odds
movement and bookmaker dispersion do not (they are confounded with
market strength), and an uncertainty index adds roughly ten
percentage points of stratification beyond market strength.
\item \textbf{Contribution 3: an uncertainty-aware abstention
framework improves decision quality.} Prediction confidence should
not be interpreted as betting edge, but uncertainty can be used to
decide when to abstain. A no-bet policy on high-uncertainty matches
improves risk-adjusted returns (error rates among high-confidence
predictions exceed the market's: 22.3\% vs 16.0\% at confidence
$\geq 0.7$), and the rule is an abstention policy in the
selective-prediction sense---trading coverage against risk rather
than against accuracy---whose threshold is a coverage control.
\item \textbf{Contribution 4: a leakage-controlled benchmark
including LLMs.} The full pipeline is public and script-driven; every
number is reproducible from raw public data. The benchmark makes the
three contributions above credible: it is the methodology that
supports the negative result on edge, the positive result on
difficulty, and the abstention framework. All financial evaluations
share one betting protocol (equal-stake and fractional-Kelly on a
single bookmaker's closing odds, bootstrap confidence intervals,
coverage reporting, and no-bet decisions separated from executed
bets).
\end{itemize}
